It does not set thresholds or propose remediation. The dataset is synthetic, generated for this exercise. This pack demonstrates a method; the figures describe this register and are not evidence about how controls behave in a real institution.

On the two deadline figures. 33 items are past due today. But 51 items missed their deadline at some point — the other 18 were completed late and then closed. The register stops counting a breach once an item closes, so the second figure is the one that describes how reliably commitments are being met. Both are shown deliberately.

The bottom line
Deadline performance is not evenly distributed and it is not random. It tracks almost entirely with whether the underlying control has been tested and found effective. Where controls are confirmed effective, items close on time 94% of the time. Where they are not — including where they have simply never been tested — items go past due roughly half the time. Nothing else in the register comes close to explaining outcomes this well: not the department, not who owns the item, not how it was identified, and not its risk tier.

One caution on the dollar figures

Financial exposure in this register is assigned in bands that follow risk tier — Critical items are always the large-dollar items by construction. Exposure is therefore a reliable measure of how much is sitting in each state, but it is not independent evidence about anything. Where a dollar figure appears in this pack, it is restating a tier figure in different units.

The obvious objection, and the answer

You could reasonably argue this is really about difficulty rather than controls: high-risk items are harder to fix, and they also tend to have weaker controls, so the rating might just be standing in for how hard the work is.

I checked. Splitting the items by risk level and looking at each level on its own, the pattern still holds — and it is widest where the stakes are highest. Among the 18 high-risk items with a control confirmed effective, none are past due. Among the 23 high-risk items without one, 15 are. The full working is in the Auditor Detail tab.

A separate issue worth flagging

17 items were closed while nobody had tested whether their control actually worked. Those closures have no evidence behind them either way.

What the data points leadership toward
  1. Treat "Not Tested" as a finding, not a placeholder. Nineteen items carrying an untested control faced a deadline and eight are past due — the same failure rate as controls already known to be weak. A further 17 items were closed with no test evidence at all. The register currently gives "Not Tested" the visual weight of a neutral status. The data does not support that treatment.
  2. The $39.8M in items older than 180 days is where attention pays. Twenty-one items hold 94.6% of past-due exposure. The recently-lapsed population is numerous but carries $2.3M in total. Effort spread evenly across all 33 past-due items would be misallocated by roughly twenty to one.
  3. Top-tier items do not slip more often — but when they slip, they stay slipped. Critical and High items go past due at broadly the same rate as Medium and Low. What separates them is duration: a median of roughly a year outstanding, against ten weeks at Low tier. This points at escalation and follow-through after a breach, not at prevention before one.
  4. Do not read department, owner or identification-source rankings as performance. Those differences are within ordinary variation for groups this size and cannot support a conclusion about any named team. Detail and testing in the Auditor tab.
  5. One register change is recommended. The system zeroes the overdue counter when an item closes, which hides every breach that was eventually cured — 18 of them here. Adding a permanent "days late at closure" field would make deadline performance visible without re-deriving it each cycle.

Scope

What this pack establishes, and what it does not

Supported

  • The current position of the register by state, item count and exposure.
  • How long past-due items have been outstanding and where the exposure concentrates.
  • That control effectiveness predicts on-time closure — and that it holds inside every risk tier examined separately.
  • That an untested control performs no better than one already found weak.
  • That 51 of 116 items with a deadline missed it, 18 of which were later closed.
  • That higher-tier items which slip stay unresolved substantially longer.

Not supported

  • Any difference by department, owner role or identification source. All three fall within ordinary variation.
  • That risk tier predicts whether an item goes past due. Critical and High are indistinguishable from each other and from the lower tiers.
  • That exposure drives outcomes. Exposure is banded from tier, so any dollar pattern restates a tier pattern.
  • Whether performance is improving. This is a single snapshot; measuring change requires a second.
  • Whether the breach rate is acceptable. No internal threshold or external benchmark exists in this data and none has been assumed.
  • What should be done. This pack describes the position. Setting tolerances and directing remediation sits with leadership.

Conventions used throughout this tab
"Faced a deadline"
An item whose Due_Date fell on or before the 30 June 2026 snapshot. Items not yet due cannot be late and are excluded from every timeliness measure. 116 of 160 items qualify.
Confidence interval (CI)
Clopper–Pearson exact 95% interval. Written "CI 25–61%". Where two groups' intervals overlap substantially, the observed difference does not support a conclusion.
"Thin base"
Fewer than 20 items in the group. Applied mechanically to every group in this pack, without exception.
"Within ordinary variation"
The between-group differences are no larger than chance allocation would routinely produce. Reported with the test statistic and p-value in each case.

The 34-item adjustment — why the on-time rate is 78.3% and not 85%

Of the 117 closed items, 34 closed before their due date had arrived. Those items had no opportunity to be late. Including them in an on-time closure rate inflates it, because they enter both numerator and denominator as automatic successes.

The on-time rate reported in this pack uses only the 83 closed items that faced a deadline, of which 65 closed on or before the due date and 18 closed late:

Known limitation in the source register — and the recommended fix

The Days_Overdue field is reset to zero when an item closes. All 18 items that closed late (1 to 59 days, median 22) carry Days_Overdue = 0. Any breach measure taken from that field alone counts only items currently past due and silently forgives every breach that was subsequently cured.

Structural dependencies between fields

Three fields in this register are not independent of one another. Any analysis treating them as separate evidence would double-count. This is stated before the exhibits because it constrains what several of them can claim.

Consequence for interpretation

Risk tier is a deterministic banding of the inherent risk score — the score ranges do not overlap at any boundary. Financial exposure is banded the same way, with near-disjoint ranges across tiers.

Therefore: exposure carries no information independent of tier. A statement such as "$24.0M of past-due exposure sits in Critical items" is a restatement of "Critical items are the expensive ones by construction." Exposure is retained throughout this pack to size the position and is never used as evidence of behaviour.

Statistical basis · Spearman rank correlation between Financial_Exposure_USD and Inherent_Risk_Score = 0.89. Tier boundaries map one-to-one onto score ranges with no overlap.

Three separate conclusions, at three different confidence levels

1. High confidence — the association exists. Across the four ratings the relationship is far stronger than chance would produce.

2. High confidence — "Not Tested" performs like "Partially Effective." 42.1% against 42.4%, with no detectable difference between them. An untested control is not behaving as a neutral or pending state in this population.

3. Low confidence on magnitude. The Effective-to-Ineffective spread is approximately fourteen-fold, but rests on a ten-item base with a confidence interval of 44–97%. A single item moving shifts the multiple between roughly twelve-fold and sixteen-fold. The direction is certain; the multiple should not be quoted as a precise figure.

Separate observation. 17 items were closed while their control was still rated Not Tested. Those closures carry no effectiveness evidence. No inference is drawn here about their failure rate: closed and past-due items are not exchangeable populations, and an item may have closed because its control functioned.

Statistical basis · Four-group association χ² = 31.87, df = 3, p = 5.6 × 10⁻⁷. Not Tested vs Partially Effective, Fisher exact p = 1.00. Effective vs Ineffective, Fisher exact p = 1.5 × 10⁻⁶. Intervals are Clopper–Pearson exact.

Why the widest-looking gap is not a finding

The largest apparent contrast here is External Regulatory Exam (46.7%) against Third-Party Review (11.8%) — a ratio of nearly four. It does not survive scrutiny, for two reasons.

Multiple comparisons. Selecting the highest and lowest of six groups and reporting the ratio between them will produce a striking-looking gap in almost any dataset. With six groups there are fifteen possible pairings. Taken alone and uncorrected the pair reaches p = 0.049; the Bonferroni-corrected threshold for fifteen comparisons is p = 0.0033, which it misses by more than an order of magnitude.

Base size. The comparison rests on seven past-due items against two. Two items moving in either group reverses the direction of the result.

Bars are drawn in a single neutral colour throughout this exhibit. A red-to-green scale would imply a ranking the data cannot support.

Statistical basis · Pearson χ² on the full contingency table for each dimension, α = 0.05. Bonferroni correction applied to all post-hoc pairwise comparisons. MAP_Linked was also tested (χ² p = 0.987) and likewise shows no association.

The overrun ratio is confounded by its own denominator

Overrun ratio is defined as Days_Overdue ÷ deadline_window, where the window runs from identification to due date. That window is strongly tier-dependent: median 259 days at Low falling to 104 days at Critical. Critical items are allowed roughly 40% of the remediation time Low items receive.

Dividing by a systematically smaller denominator inflates the ratio for higher tiers irrespective of behaviour — and here the distortion is large enough to reverse the ranking. The ratio places Critical (2.94×) above High (2.20×). Measured in raw days, the order inverts: High items sit a median 410 days past due against Critical's 302.

What survives. Raw days past due does differ materially across tiers, rising from 75 days at Low to 302–410 days at the top two tiers. The defensible formulation is: tier does not predict whether an item slips, but top-tier items that slip remain unresolved substantially longer. The overrun ratio column is retained for completeness and should not be cited.

Raw days past due across tiers (past-due items only), Kruskal–Wallis p = 0.005. Overrun figures are medians of per-item ratios, not ratios of medians.

Scope

What this pack establishes, and what it does not

Supported by this analysis

  • The current position of the register by state, item count and exposure, fully reconciled in Section A.
  • How long past-due items have been outstanding and where exposure concentrates within that profile, with the age-dependence of status confirmed.
  • That control effectiveness rating is associated with on-time closure (χ² p < 0.000001), and that the association holds after stratification by risk tier (CMH p < 0.00001).
  • That "Not Tested" performs indistinguishably from "Partially Effective" (Fisher p = 1.00).
  • That 51 of 116 deadline-facing items breached, of which 18 were subsequently closed.
  • That raw days past due rises with tier (Kruskal–Wallis p = 0.005).

Not supported — and why

  • Any difference by identification source, department, owner role, risk domain or MAP linkage. All test null; see Exhibit A4 for statistics.
  • That risk tier predicts whether an item goes past due (χ² p = 0.43).
  • That the overrun ratio measures behaviour. Its denominator is tier-dependent and the confound reverses the Critical/High ranking.
  • That exposure drives outcomes. Exposure is banded from tier with near-disjoint ranges; dollar concentration restates tier concentration.
  • Whether performance is improving. Single cross-sectional snapshot; period-over-period comparison requires a second observation.
  • Whether the breach rate is acceptable. No internal threshold or external benchmark exists in this data and none has been assumed.
  • What should be done. This pack describes the position. Setting tolerances and directing remediation sits with leadership.
